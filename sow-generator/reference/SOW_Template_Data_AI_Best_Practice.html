How to read this document

This is a dual-layer reference: client-facing clause text (white contract cards, numbered per section) is interleaved with Drafting Guidance (gold-bordered callouts) explaining why a clause exists and what to watch for. Strip every gold callout before sending an SOW to a client — they are for the drafting team only.

Why Data/AI/Knowledge SOWs Are Different

Seven structural risks make this category harder to put on fixed-fee/outcome-based terms than a standard IT delivery SOW — everything downstream is built to manage these explicitly.

1. Unknown inputs at signature

Scope is set against data you haven't yet profiled for quality or completeness — the single biggest source of fixed-fee margin erosion.

2. Probabilistic outcomes

An AI model or analytics finding can't be warranted "correct" like a CRUD feature. Outcome fees must pin to thresholds, not open promises.

3. Advisory bleeds into delivery

Assessment/strategy work shades into build without a clean boundary unless the SOW forces a re-scoping gate.

4. Third-party model/platform dependency

Hyperscaler/LLM pricing, rate limits, and deprecations sit outside your control and must be flowed through, not absorbed.

5. Entangled IP

Client data, client IP, your reusable accelerators, model weights, and prompts each need separate ownership treatment.

6. Growing regulatory exposure

GDPR, EU AI Act risk-tiering, sector rules, and client responsible-AI policies all bear on scope and liability.

7. Client-side dependencies dominate the critical path

Data access approvals, SME availability, and decision latency are usually the real gating factor — a fixed-fee/outcome-based deal that doesn't tie price and timeline to client obligations is a deal where you eat the risk of someone else's delay.

Drafting Guidance

An Executive Summary is optional for very short SOWs but recommended by default — it makes the document read as a live proposal rather than a static legal form, and gives a senior sponsor who won't read the clauses a reason to engage with the rest. Drafting Guidance

Business Context, Objectives, and Scope sit together deliberately: a reviewer should see what problem this solves, what "done" looks like, and exactly what's in and out, in one continuous read — before hitting any technical or commercial detail. Put out-of-scope immediately after in-scope, not at the end of the document, so it can't be missed.

PoC / pilot storytelling. If this SOW covers a proof of concept or pilot, Section 3.1 must explicitly place it inside a larger narrative: what this phase is intended to prove, what target end-state it feeds into, and what decision it unlocks if successful. Don't let a PoC read as an isolated, disconnected exercise — tie every deliverable back to something strategic.

3.3 vs. 3.4 vs. 3.7 — keep these conceptually distinct. Assumptions are things you are pricing on the belief they're true (e.g., data volume). Prerequisites are a dated, checkable readiness gate before work starts (access, hardware, SME confirmation) — important enough that it also belongs in the Executive Summary, since a missed prerequisite is the single most common cause of a slipped kickoff date. Dependencies are outside both parties' control — flagging them doesn't remove risk, but it does mean a dependency failure is a Change Control trigger (Section 9) rather than a silent absorption of delay cost.

Drafting Guidance — four things that make a metric enforceable
  • Fixed test/evaluation set, agreed and frozen before build starts. If the client can change the test set after seeing results, it isn't a metric.
  • A stated baseline. "30% cycle-time reduction" is meaningless without an agreed pre-engagement baseline measurement method and date.
  • A measurement window with a hard stop. Otherwise "we'll keep measuring adoption" becomes unbounded warranty.
  • Named attribution of confounds — state the metric is measured "assuming no material change to [upstream data feed / organizational process / staffing]."

Never use unbounded/comparative language ("better," "more accurate," "state of the art") as the metric itself — only as color commentary around a numeric target.

5.1 Acceptance procedure.

  • Cegeka delivers and notifies Client in writing.
  • Client reviews against the stated acceptance criteria only (not new/undocumented criteria) within a standard 3 business day review window (see drafting guidance below).
  • Silence beyond the window = deemed acceptance.
  • Rejection must be in writing, citing the specific unmet criterion, within the window.
  • [N] rounds of rework are included per deliverable for defects against stated criteria; issues outside the stated criteria are handled via Change Control (Section 9).

5.2 Progress checkpoint (value gate). At an agreed point in the engagement (e.g., approximately [two-thirds] of elapsed duration or spend), the parties hold a joint checkpoint to confirm the engagement remains on track to meet the Success Metrics in Section 4. The checkpoint outcome (continue / adjust / stop) and its consequence for remaining fees are recorded in writing; detailed operational governance mechanics for running this checkpoint sit in the project's own governance cadence (Section 6.5), not in this SOW.

Drafting Guidance

Standardize the review window at 3 business days, not 10. A 10-day acceptance window (a common default carried over from some hyperscaler/vendor paper) is too long for a fixed-price engagement rhythm — it lets deemed-acceptance dates drift and stretches the whole delivery cadence. Three business days keeps momentum and still gives the client a genuine chance to review; extend it in writing on a specific deliverable if the content warrants it, rather than defaulting every deliverable to a long window.

For AI/analytics deliverables, acceptance criteria must be pre-agreed and specific enough that a third party could adjudicate pass/fail without either party's testimony. Avoid criteria like "client is satisfied with the model" — replace with "model achieves the thresholds in Section 4 on the frozen test set in Appendix B." For advisory deliverables, tie acceptance to presentation and coverage of agreed topics, not to the client agreeing with the conclusions.

Every deliverable maps to exactly one invoicing milestone and a specific target date — carrying the date as its own column (rather than leaving it only in the Timeline table) makes the deliverable-to-payment linkage visible on the same page a client finance reviewer is looking at.

Strategic Synthesis: The gating mechanism reconciles "fixed fee" with unknown data quality

You are not quoting one number for an unknown; you are quoting a small fixed fee for the phase that removes the unknown (Discovery), then re-quoting the next phase with better information. Selling this structure to clients who want one number up front is a commercial conversation, not a drafting problem — but it is the single highest-leverage practice for protecting margin on data/AI fixed-fee deals, and worth defending in negotiation.

6.3 Technical description & reference architecture. Describe the target technical/architecture area at a level sufficient for the client's architecture board to review, including: platform(s) used (e.g., Azure, Databricks, Snowflake), the reference architecture pattern applied, integration points, and any deviations from Cegeka's standard reference architecture with rationale.

6.4 Governance & reporting cadence. Steering committee frequency, status reporting format, and escalation path (Section 8). In addition to phase gates (6.2), maintain a recurring operational status checkpoint (e.g., weekly or bi-weekly) reporting simple on-track/off-track (RAG) status against plan. A brief client satisfaction (NPS) pulse may optionally be collected at phase transitions.

6.5 Phase kick-off briefings. Each phase in 6.1 opens with a short briefing/knowledge-transfer session (train-the-trainer where relevant to the Client team) that: (a) confirms scope and expectations for the phase about to start, (b) marks a clear, unambiguous boundary between phases, and (c) supports the approval and invoicing trigger associated with the prior phase's completion (Section 12.2).

Drafting Guidance

Keep 6.1 to phase names and gates — a full delivery-methodology description (e.g., a CRISP-DM walkthrough for an AI project) does not belong inside the SOW itself. If a methodology deep-dive adds value for the client, deliver it as a separate presentation/collateral; the SOW's job is to fix scope, gates, and fees, not to teach methodology.

Hypercare is conditional on project type. A hypercare (elevated post-go-live support) phase applies to industrialization/production-rollout engagements. It does not apply to a PoC or an advisory-only engagement — remove the row entirely for those project types rather than leaving it in as unused boilerplate. Which optional phases apply should be driven by the engagement's project type classification at the point the SOW is drafted.

6.5's phase-opening briefings are a small addition with a disproportionate payoff on fixed-price work: they create a hard, mutually-acknowledged line ("we are now leaving Design and entering Build") that both sides can point back to, which is exactly the moment acceptance, sign-off, and invoicing should trigger — rather than relying on an ambiguous sense that a phase has "basically" finished.

The recurring RAG status checkpoint in 6.4 is distinct from, and cheaper than, the formal phase gates in 6.2 — it's a lightweight cadence that surfaces drift early. This is especially valuable on fixed-price engagements: a client who is told "we are off track" at week 3 can help unblock a dependency; a client who finds out at final delivery has grounds for a dispute instead.

Drafting Guidance

The 7.4 clause is what stops "client dependency risk" from being invisible until it's a margin problem. Put a number on the grace period and state the remedy now, not when it's already happened.

Never leave "timely manner" undefined. Any obligation phrased as "in a timely manner" or "within a reasonable time" should be replaced with a specific business-day count wherever it appears in this document (Sections 3.4, 5.1, 7.2). This single wording habit is disproportionately important — vague timing language is one of the most common sources of client-obligation disputes on fixed-price engagements.

9. Change Control

  1. Either party may raise a Change Request (CR) using the template in Appendix E when: (a) a Section 3.3 assumption proves false, (b) new scope is requested, (c) a Section 7.2 Client obligation or Section 3.4 Prerequisite is missed beyond the grace period, (d) a Section 3.7 Dependency is not resolved in time and materially affects scope or timeline, or (e) a Success Metric definition needs to change.
  2. Cegeka provides a CR impact assessment (scope, fee, timeline) within 5 business days.
  3. No work on the CR begins, and no scope/fee/timeline is deemed changed, until both parties sign the CR.
  4. Absent an approved CR, the original SOW scope, fee, and acceptance criteria remain binding on both parties.
Drafting Guidance

A fixed-fee SOW is only as strong as its change control discipline. The most common real-world failure isn't a missing clause — it's a delivery team verbally agreeing to "just one more thing" in a workshop without running it through this process. State in the kickoff deck (not just the contract) that all scope changes go through Appendix E, and name who on your side has authority to approve informal scope drift (ideally: nobody).

State clearly which model applies — this single field determines which fee mechanics in Section 12 are load-bearing.

Fixed Fee — Scope, deliverables, and acceptance criteria in Sections 3, 5 and 6 are fixed; price does not vary with effort.

Time & Materials (reference only — not the default for this template)
Drafting Guidance

This clause is placed immediately before Commercial Terms deliberately — it has nothing to do with the Success Metrics, deliverables, acceptance criteria, or approach/methodology defined earlier; it is purely a commercial classification that determines how Section 12 is read. Pick one honestly: the most common failure mode in this space is calling something "fixed fee" while the actual scope is written in outcome language with no measurable finish line — that's a T&M engagement wearing a fixed-fee price tag. If it's genuinely outcome-based, Section 4 is not optional — it is the contract.

Risk: Uncapped LLM/compute pass-through

Do not fold variable LLM/compute consumption into a fixed fee unless you have hard usage caps in the assumptions (Section 3.3) and a metering/alerting mechanism. Drafting Guidance

Missing invoicing details (12.7) are one of the most common causes of late payment that have nothing to do with the client disputing the work — collect the billing contact, invoice email, and PO reference at signature, not after the first invoice bounces.

Drafting Guidance

Signatories and day-to-day contacts are usually different people and should be captured separately. Signatories are often senior and reasonably decline to share a direct phone number — don't require it. The operational/invoicing contact is who actually handles the relationship day to day and processes invoices, so capture full contact details including phone for that person on both sides. Missing invoicing contact details (also see Section 12.7) is a routine, avoidable cause of late payment.

Pre-Flight Checklist Before Sending an SOW in This Category

Executive Summary is a page or less, states the pre-kickoff planning session and the 5-business-day prerequisites lead time, and every claim in it is traceable to a numbered section
Business Context/Objectives tags each objective tactical or strategic; PoC engagements include the "step one of a larger journey" narrative
Out-of-scope sits immediately after in-scope, not at the back of the document
Section 3.4 Prerequisites and Section 3.7 Dependencies are both present and kept conceptually distinct from Section 3.3 Assumptions
